1. Purpose and Scope

The client has requested a bespoke reinsurance reconciliation platform — the full-platform option (Option 2, approximately $758K, 9.5 months) recommended in the solution design. This document takes a step back and asks two questions the client did not ask: are there alternative ways to reach the same business goals, and are there scope extensions that could deliver additional value beyond what was requested?

The audience is the solution architect and engagement lead. The purpose is to ensure that the recommended approach has been tested against the realistic alternative paths before client conversations begin, and to identify extensions worth surfacing during the Investigation Period.

The three business goals being optimised throughout this analysis are: (1) achieve a 2–4× headcount reduction (from ~100 reconciliation managers to 25–50); (2) enable scalable counterparty onboarding without proportional staffing growth; (3) satisfy UK FCA and Solvency II record-keeping obligations with a tamper-evident, machine-readable audit trail.

2. Evaluation Framework

2.1. Evaluation Dimensions

Every alternative and scope extension is assessed across five dimensions. A shorthand rating (High / Medium / Low) is used where a precise figure is not available.

Dimension What it measures

Business goal coverage

Does this option achieve headcount reduction, scalable counterparty onboarding, and Solvency II-grade auditability — fully, partially, or not at all?

Delivery risk

Complexity, vendor dependency, timeline uncertainty, and integration unknowns that could cause delay or cost overrun relative to the baseline bespoke build.

Cost profile

CAPEX vs. OPEX tradeoff; order-of-magnitude comparison to the $758K bespoke baseline; sensitivity to counterparty volume.

Trust architecture fit

Can this option provide the custodial, tamper-evident trust layer that the domain requires — where the reinsurer is the single authoritative source of record for inter-company reconciliation?

Strategic optionality

Does this option open or close future value? For example, does it enable commercialisation, ML analytics, or event-driven reconciliation — or does it foreclose them?

2.2. Recommendation Vocabulary

Each alternative and extension receives one of four labels. The label reflects the conclusion of the analysis, not a preference.

Label Meaning

Pursue

Replace or substantially supplement the recommended Option 2 solution.

Extend

Add to the current engagement scope as an increment — either in Phase 4 or as a Phase 1 investigation work item.

Defer

Worth doing, but not now. A specific trigger condition (data volume, counterparty count, post-launch stability) must be met first.

Reject

Not appropriate for this context. The rationale explains why.

3. Alternative Solutions

3.1. Buy vs. Build: Procure a Commercial Insurance/Reinsurance SaaS Platform

3.1.1. Description

Rather than building a bespoke platform, procure an existing insurance or reinsurance administration SaaS product from the market. Candidates include Majesco (cloud insurance SaaS), Duck Creek Technologies (P&C-focused), Sapiens (insurance and reinsurance administration), FIS (reinsurance management suites), and specialist longevity/life reinsurance platforms from vendors such as RGA Technology Partners or Gen Re.

3.1.2. Analysis

The central fit problem is that most insurance SaaS is built for the cedent — the primary insurer that cedes risk to a reinsurer. Platforms designed for the reinsurer as the data custodian and computation authority, receiving monthly mortality files from multiple counterparties and producing compensation calculations, are rare. The property/casualty and quota-share markets are well served; longevity risk reconciliation at the individual-insured data-file level is a niche.

The two capabilities that are most likely to be absent or only partially configurable in commercial platforms are: (a) per-counterparty file normalisation with arbitrary column mappings, and (b) an expression-tree eligibility engine with exact decimal arithmetic enforced. Most commercial platforms offer rule engines, but the precision requirements of longevity contracts — where rounding errors compound over hundreds of thousands of individuals — are unusually strict.

Pros: * Faster time to value for standard contract types; vendor manages infrastructure, upgrades, and compliance certifications. * Lower internal build risk — no greenfield engineering. * Regulatory compliance features (Solvency II reporting, audit trails) may already be certified.

Cons: * High probability of a fit gap in the custodial trust architecture; most platforms assume neutral custody or carrier ownership, not reinsurer-as-custodian. * Per-seat or per-policy pricing models typically exceed bespoke TCO once counterparty volume grows beyond 10–15. * Data residency requirements and KMS key ownership are frequently non-negotiable in multi-tenant SaaS. * Vendor lock-in: contract rules encoded in a proprietary rule engine cannot be migrated without rebuilding.

Feasibility: A full commercial fit is unlikely, but a partial fit is possible — a commercial platform for contract registry, counterparty management, and regulatory reporting, with a bespoke eligibility engine and audit ledger layered on top. This hybrid would reduce bespoke scope by approximately 25–35%.

3.1.3. Recommendation: Defer

Add a commercial reinsurance SaaS market scan as an Investigation Period work item (2–3 weeks, architect-led). A complete replacement is unlikely, but a hybrid architecture may be viable and could reduce bespoke development cost. Do not delay the engagement waiting for this scan; run it in parallel with Phase 1 domain workshops.

3.2. Low-Code / RPA: Automate the Existing Excel and Email Process

3.2.1. Description

Use Robotic Process Automation tooling (UiPath, Automation Anywhere, or Microsoft Power Automate) to automate the manual steps of the current Excel-and-email workflow without building a new platform. Automation would cover file receipt routing, Excel macro execution, email dispatch for approvals, and basic audit logging.

3.2.2. Analysis

RPA automates process steps; it does not solve the trust problem. The platform’s core value is not speed — it is that both parties agree to a single canonical computation backed by a tamper-evident record. An email that says "the UiPath bot ran the macro and it says £45,230" is epistemically identical to a human running the macro. No regulatory body would accept RPA-over-Excel as a Solvency II-compliant audit trail.

The headcount impact ceiling is also structurally limited. RPA eliminates some steps but does not eliminate roles. The 100 reconciliation managers exist because the process requires human verification, dispute management, and relationship management — none of which RPA addresses. A realistic RPA-only outcome is a 10–20% efficiency gain per manager, not a 2–4× headcount reduction.

A further risk: RPA layers automation onto a fragile Excel-based process. New counterparty file formats, Excel version changes, or formula updates break the automation without warning. The automation becomes a maintenance burden layered on top of the existing fragility.

Pros: * Very low upfront cost (estimated £40–80K, 2–4 months). * No counterparty change management required. * Immediate productivity gain for the existing team. * Could reduce cycle time even without structural change.

Cons: * Does not achieve the 2–4× headcount reduction target. * Does not solve the audit evidence problem. * Does not satisfy Solvency II intent (automation of a non-auditable process is not an auditable process). * Cannot support counterparty portfolio growth without proportional headcount growth. * Creates a new fragility layer on top of existing fragility.

Feasibility: High as a short-term bridge; low as a strategic solution.

3.2.3. Recommendation: Reject as primary solution

However: a targeted RPA layer for file routing, notification dispatch, and cycle status emails could serve as a 3–6 month bridge while the platform is built, reducing manual overhead during the delivery period. If the client has an immediate operational pressure (e.g., counterparty growth before the platform is ready), this is worth raising in the Investigation Period kickoff.

3.3. API-First Counterparty Integration: Direct System-to-System Data Exchange

3.3.1. Description

Rather than counterparties uploading monthly Excel/CSV files via a portal, integrate directly with each counterparty’s policy administration or actuarial system via API. The reconciliation data would be pushed or pulled programmatically — the file normalisation layer becomes an API adapter layer, and the ingestion pipeline becomes event-triggered rather than file-triggered.

3.3.2. Analysis

This option changes the architecture at the ingestion boundary, not in the core platform. The eligibility engine, audit ledger, and approval workflow remain identical. The change is: instead of a human at the counterparty uploading a file through the portal, the counterparty system calls a REST endpoint (or the platform polls the counterparty system) and submits a structured payload.

The constraint is counterparty system maturity. UK and European insurers span a wide range — from modern cloud-native platforms (Azure, AWS, Salesforce Financial Services) to mainframe-era policy administration systems (COBOL-based, no API layer). The counterparties with the highest reconciliation volumes and the most to gain from integration are also often the largest and most likely to have legacy systems. Requiring API integration as a condition of onboarding would significantly increase the onboarding barrier and exclude a subset of counterparties entirely.

A hybrid model — API for capable counterparties, file upload for legacy — adds architectural complexity (two ingestion paths) without eliminating the file-based path.

Pros: * Eliminates manual data preparation on the counterparty side. * Reduces data quality errors from manual file construction. * Enables near-real-time data freshness; sets up event-driven reconciliation (see Section 4.2). * Reduces counterparty operational overhead over time.

Cons: * Many counterparties cannot provide APIs at launch; integration work falls to the counterparty, creating a change management and procurement challenge. * Onboarding complexity increases substantially for API-capable counterparties. * Hybrid model adds two ingestion paths to maintain.

Feasibility: Low for most counterparties at launch; High for tier-1 counterparties with modern systems within 12–18 months.

3.3.3. Recommendation: Defer

Design the file normalisation layer behind a FileNormaliser interface so that an API-based adapter can be added later without rebuilding the ingestion pipeline. This costs nothing during Phase 1. Revisit for tier-1 counterparties at the Phase 4 retrospective once their system integration maturity has been assessed during onboarding.

3.4. Federated Data Model: Shared Canonical Data Space

3.4.1. Description

Rather than the reinsurer acting as the sole custodian of reconciliation data, both parties write to a shared canonical data space — a neutral ledger where neither party is the single owner. Implementation options range from a shared-cloud workspace (cross-account S3 replication, a neutral Aurora instance) to a distributed ledger (permissioned blockchain such as Hyperledger Fabric).

3.4.2. Analysis

This option misunderstands the trust architecture of the domain. The reinsurer is already the de facto custodian of the reconciliation process: they hold the contracts, produce the calculations, and carry regulatory responsibility under FCA and Solvency II. A federated model introduces either a neutral third party (who governs it? who pays?) or a genuinely distributed trust anchor (blockchain). Neither fits the contractual and regulatory model.

The solution design’s ADR-001 addresses this directly (Architecture Decisions section). The blockchain sub-case was evaluated and rejected on three grounds: (a) the reinsurer is already the single custodian — distributed consensus adds no value in a single-custodian model; (b) QLDB was discontinued in 2025; (c) immuDB had transaction consistency issues at scale with non-Go clients. A shared-cloud (non-blockchain) federation introduces data residency, access control, and billing complications without solving the underlying trust question.

The SHA-256 hash chain with KMS-signed genesis — the approach in the recommended design — already provides independently verifiable tamper-evidence without requiring federated control. If a specific counterparty challenges custodianship, the audit ledger provides the same cryptographic guarantee that a blockchain would, without consortium governance overhead.

Pros: * Higher counterparty trust if custodianship is disputed. * Regulatory preference for neutral records in some jurisdictions (though the UK FCA does not mandate this).

Cons: * Significant governance overhead — who operates the neutral node? * The reinsurer loses control of their own platform and data. * Does not match the contractual model; counterparties accept the reinsurer’s calculations, not a neutral third party’s. * Higher latency for dispute resolution (consensus required before data can be acted on).

3.4.3. Recommendation: Reject

The custodial model is correct for this domain. The hash-chain audit ledger with KMS-signed genesis achieves equivalent tamper-evidence without federated governance. If a regulatory change mandates neutral custody in future, the audit ledger’s architecture is already the closest available foundation.

4. Scope Extensions

4.1. AI-Augmented Reconciliation

Three distinct AI/ML capabilities are worth separating — they have different prerequisites, timelines, and ROI profiles.

4.1.1. LLM-Assisted Rule Extraction from Legacy Excel

What it adds: An LLM-assisted tooling layer that parses legacy Excel workbooks and proposes structured contract rule definitions in the platform’s expression-tree format. The architect reviews and validates each proposed rule against historical outputs. This directly addresses the key-person risk listed in the risk register: formula knowledge currently locked in personal Excel files held by a small number of experienced reconciliation managers.

Prerequisites: Access to legacy Excel workbooks (obtained during Investigation Period domain workshops); a mandatory human review gate — LLM output is a starting draft, not a production rule. The rule is not activated until it has been validated against historical cycle outputs (side-by-side comparison).

Effort: 2–3 months (tooling, extraction workflow, validation harness).

Recommendation: Extend. This should be proposed as a Phase 1 investigation work item — run the extraction during the domain knowledge workshops, not as a separate phase. The ROI is high (eliminates months of manual rule transcription) and the risk is low (the validation gate means incorrect LLM output cannot reach production). Include in the Investigation Period scope.

4.1.2. ML Anomaly Detection

What it adds: Statistical baseline modelling per counterparty portfolio; flag mortality submissions that deviate significantly from historical norms before they enter the eligibility engine. Surfaces data quality errors (incorrect records, missing deaths) and potentially fraudulent submissions without requiring a human to review every file.

Prerequisites: 12+ months of per-counterparty historical data; an analytics read replica (not the operational database); an anonymisation pipeline if aggregating across counterparties (GDPR review required).

Effort: 3–4 months (ML pipeline, model training, threshold calibration, alert integration).

Recommendation: Defer. Viable at 18–24 months post-launch once sufficient per-counterparty history exists. The platform architecture (immutable ledger, structured event history, structured file uploads) is already the right foundation. Document as a roadmap item.

4.1.3. Predictive Dispute Analytics

What it adds: Score each reconciliation cycle at proposal time with a dispute probability indicator, based on historical patterns: counterparty track record, data quality score, contract complexity, and compensation amount relative to historical variance. Helps reconciliation managers prioritise attention before disputes are raised.

Prerequisites: 6+ months of cycle outcome data with dispute flags.

Effort: 2 months.

Recommendation: Defer. Lower priority than ML anomaly detection; revisit at Phase 4 retrospective once dispute data is accumulating.

4.2. Continuous / Event-Driven Reconciliation

4.2.1. Description

Replace the monthly batch model with event-driven reconciliation triggered by individual mortality events — death notifications from a life events data feed (e.g., ONS mortality data, industry feeds). Each event triggers an eligibility check in near-real time; the monthly cycle becomes a settlement confirmation rather than a calculation event.

4.2.2. Analysis

This is architecturally significant. The Step Functions approval workflow would need to be supplemented with an event bus (AWS EventBridge or Kafka). The eligibility engine must handle idempotent, late-arriving events. The audit ledger model already supports this (event-oriented, append-only) — the ingestion and workflow layers are the constraint.

The larger constraint is counterparty readiness. The monthly file model is the path of least resistance for all counterparty sizes and system maturities. Event-driven reconciliation requires counterparties to expose mortality event feeds — a significantly higher integration bar than uploading a monthly CSV. Most UK/European insurers cannot provide this today.

The platform architecture should be designed to support this evolution — the Step Functions / event-driven model and the audit ledger are already oriented this way — but requiring it at launch is not feasible.

Recommendation: Defer. 2–3 year strategic horizon. Ensure no design decision in Phases 1–4 forecloses event-driven ingestion (the FileNormaliser interface abstraction covers this). Revisit when 2+ counterparties with modern event-capable systems are live on the platform.

4.3. Platform Commercialisation

4.3.1. Description

After internal deployment is stable, white-label the platform and offer it to other longevity reinsurers as a SaaS product. The multi-tenant architecture — RLS-enforced tenant isolation, per-tenant contract rules, per-counterparty file normalisation, per-tenant data residency zones — is already the architecture of a SaaS product.

4.3.2. Analysis

No other UK reinsurer currently operates a digital reconciliation platform of this kind. The market gap is real. The multi-tenant design means that adding a second reinsurer as a "super-tenant" (with their own counterparty tenants) is an architectural extension, not a rebuild.

What commercialisation requires that the base platform does not have:

  • Self-service tenant onboarding — currently an operational task (manual provisioning by the reinsurer’s platform ops team); SaaS requires a self-service flow.

  • Usage metering and billing integration — monthly per-cycle or per-counterparty pricing.

  • Product-grade SLAs — 99.9% uptime SLA requires additional redundancy and incident response structure.

  • Compliance certification — SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 certification is likely required by prospective SaaS customers' vendor risk programmes.

None of these should be built in the initial engagement. The discipline is: avoid design decisions that foreclose them (no client-specific hardcoding, metered usage logging from day one, clean tenant abstraction). The commercialisation path should be explicitly noted in the solution design’s evolution roadmap so the client understands the option exists.

Recommendation: Extend — as an architectural flag only, not a deliverable. Include in the Post-Launch Plan of the solution design. The client should be aware of this option; it reframes the platform investment as a potential revenue asset, not only a cost reduction.

4.4. Counterparty Self-Service Data Quality Portal

4.4.1. Description

A pre-submission portal for counterparties to run the platform’s validation rules against their file before the official upload cycle opens. The portal returns a quality report — schema conformance, completeness checks, sanity checks against contract expectations — allowing counterparties to self-remediate before entering the reconciliation cycle.

4.4.2. Analysis

The current design applies file normalisation and schema validation after the counterparty uploads. Validation failures or data quality issues are surfaced during cycle processing, which may already be in progress. Moving the validation gate upstream — to before the official submission — reduces dispute rates, reduces reprocessing cycles, and positions the reinsurer as a collaborative partner rather than a gatekeeper.

Implementation is additive: expose a subset of the eligibility engine’s validation logic as a read-only API endpoint. The counterparty calls it with a test file and receives a structured quality report. No audit ledger entry is created (this is not a submission). The portal UI is a light wrapper on this API.

Prerequisites: Phase 2 ingestion and normalisation pipeline complete; validation logic decomposed into a reusable, read-only API function.

Effort: Approximately 2 months additional work.

Recommendation: Extend — include in Phase 4 scope alongside regulatory export. High ROI relative to effort; reduces ongoing operational burden for the platform ops team (fewer resubmission cycles to manage). The architecture design in Phase 2 should ensure the normalisation and validation logic is callable as a standalone function (not embedded in the ingestion event handler).

4.5. Longevity Analytics Dashboard

4.5.1. Description

An aggregated, anonymised analytics view across all counterparty portfolios: mortality rate trends, longevity risk concentration, portfolio-level anomalies, and compensation patterns over time. A read-only internal dashboard for the reinsurer’s actuarial and risk management teams.

4.5.2. Analysis

The platform accumulates data that is uniquely valuable in aggregate: structured, timestamped mortality status records across all counterparty portfolios. No other source provides this view to the reinsurer’s actuarial team today. Once anonymised and aggregated, this dataset enables better contract pricing, early detection of systemic longevity shifts, and an eventual anonymised data product.

The technical requirements are distinct from the operational platform: a separate analytics store (read replica or dedicated analytics database), an anonymisation pipeline, and a dashboarding layer (Apache Superset, Metabase, or Looker). This is architecturally separate from the operational platform.

The primary prerequisite concern is GDPR: individual-level mortality data originates from EU and UK counterparties and references named individuals. Aggregating across counterparties — even with anonymisation — requires legal review of data processing agreements and the reinsurer’s DPA with each counterparty.

Effort: 3–4 months.

Recommendation: Defer. 12–18 months post-launch, once data volume is sufficient and the operational platform is stable. The GDPR/DPA question must be resolved before any aggregation pipeline is designed. Flag this as a post-launch roadmap item.

5.2. Recommended Shortlist

The following is the prioritised list of actions, in order of urgency and ROI:

  1. Proceed with the bespoke platform (Option 2) as recommended. No alternative replaces it. The alternatives analysis confirms that the buy-vs-build case does not close, RPA is structurally insufficient, and the federated model is incorrect for this domain. The recommended design stands.

  2. Extend Phase 1: LLM-assisted rule extraction. Add this as an Investigation Period work item alongside domain knowledge workshops. High ROI, low risk (mandatory human review gate). This is the single highest-value addition relative to effort.

  3. Extend Phase 4: Counterparty self-service data quality portal. ~2 months additional effort; moves the quality gate upstream; reduces dispute rates and resubmission cycles from day one. Include in Phase 4 scope during contract finalisation.

  4. Flag: Design for commercialisation optionality. No additional deliverable. Three design disciplines applied during Phases 1–4 preserve the option at zero cost: no client-specific hardcoding, FileNormaliser interface abstraction, metered usage logging from day one.

  5. Defer: ML anomaly detection. Revisit at Phase 4 retrospective; requires 12+ months of data. The architecture is already the right foundation.

  6. Defer: Predictive dispute analytics. Lower priority than ML anomaly detection; revisit post Phase 4.

  7. Defer: Continuous / event-driven reconciliation. 2–3 year horizon. Ensure FileNormaliser interface abstraction does not foreclose event-driven ingestion.

  8. Defer: API-first counterparty integration. Design the normalisation layer as an interface (covered above); revisit for tier-1 counterparties at Phase 4 retrospective.

  9. Defer: Buy vs. Build market scan. Run during Investigation Period as a parallel work item; 2–3 weeks, architect-led. A hybrid may reduce bespoke scope.

  10. Reject: RPA as primary solution. Valid as a short-term bridge only. Raise with the client if there is immediate operational pressure before the platform is ready.

  11. Reject: Federated data model. The custodial model is correct. The hash-chain ledger with KMS-signed genesis is sufficient.
